# Terrarium and glass-vessel diagnosis

A plant in a terrarium is in a different world from a plant in a pot, and diagnosing it with pot
logic gets it badly wrong. The two reversals that matter most:

- **Condensation replaces the finger test.** In a closed terrarium the glass tells you the water
  balance at a glance. You will usually never touch the soil.
- **Advice inverts.** In a pot, "water it" is the common fix. In a closed terrarium, adding water is
  the most common way people kill the thing — the water cycles and never leaves, so the usual
  problem is far too much, not too little.

**First, decide which kind of vessel you're looking at**, because they behave oppositely:

| | **Closed** (lid, cork, sealed jar) | **Open** (open bowl, unsealed globe) |
|---|---|---|
| Water cycle | Self-recycling, near-sealed | Evaporates normally |
| Humidity | Very high, ~90%+ | Room humidity, slightly raised |
| Watering | Weeks to **months**; often none for a year | Occasional, small measured amounts |
| Main risk | **Mould, rot, no airflow** | Drying out; still no drainage |
| Right plants | Fittonia, moss, ferns, peperomia, pilea, small begonia, cryptanthus, syngonium | Succulents, cacti, air plants, haworthia |
| Wrong plants | Succulents and cacti — **they rot within weeks** | Moisture-lovers — they crisp |

**A succulent or cactus inside a sealed terrarium is a setup error, not a care error.** Say so
plainly — no watering schedule fixes it, and the plant will keep rotting until it's moved.

---

## 1. Read the glass first

The condensation pattern is the primary diagnostic, equivalent to soil moisture in a pot.

| What the glass shows | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Light droplets on the **upper third**, clearing through the day | **Balanced** — this is the target | Nothing. Don't water |
| Heavy fog, glass opaque, can't see in | **Too wet** — the commonest fault, especially in new builds | Open the lid for 24 h, close, reassess; repeat as needed. Don't add water |
| Persistent running water down the glass, pooling at the base | Badly overwatered | Air it out for several days; if the drainage layer is submerged, rebuild |
| **No condensation at all** + plants limp | Too dry — rare, but real | Mist lightly, a little at a time, and recheck in a day |
| Fog only in the morning, clear by midday | Normal daily cycle | Nothing |
| Green film on the inside of the glass | Algae — too much light and/or nutrients | Move out of direct sun, wipe the glass |

Also read the **drainage layer** if it's visible through the glass — a gravel or LECA layer with
standing water visible in it means the reservoir is full and the soil above is waterlogged. That's
a strong finding and easy to see in a photo of a clear vessel.

## 2. Photographing through glass — what it does to the reading

Glass adds its own distortions on top of whatever `photo-conditions.md` covers. Account for these
before diagnosing:

- **Reflections** — the glass mirrors the room. Bright patches that appear *on top of* leaves and
  don't follow leaf contours are reflections, not lesions. A reflection often shows a straight edge
  or a recognisable shape from the room; a lesion follows leaf tissue.
- **Green/blue tint** — thick or recycled glass has its own colour cast, on top of the lamp's. Use
  a neutral reference **inside** the vessel if there is one (a white pebble, pale sand, the LECA
  layer) rather than something outside the glass.
- **Condensation blur** — fog softens everything. Fine detail like early mould, stippling or thrips
  scarring simply isn't resolvable through fogged glass. Say so and ask for a photo taken shortly
  after opening the lid, once the glass has cleared.
- **Curvature** — round vessels distort shape and size near the edges. Judge leaf shape from plants
  near the centre; don't call a leaf deformed if it's pressed against curved glass.
- **Leaves pressed flat against the glass** — looks like deformity, is just contact. It does
  genuinely cause rot spots where the leaf stays wet against the glass, so it's worth mentioning as
  a cause, not as a symptom of disease.

## 3. The cleanup crew is not a pest problem

Most closed terrariums are deliberately **bioactive**: springtails and isopods are **added on
purpose** to eat mould and decaying matter. They are the terrarium's immune system, people buy them
deliberately, and finding them means the system is working.

**Never report springtails or isopods as an infestation.** For a closed terrarium, treat "bioactive
until shown otherwise" as the default assumption — it's the norm, not the exception.

**Expect them to be invisible in photos, and read nothing into that.** They live in the substrate,
under leaf litter and hardscape, and are largely nocturnal — a perfectly healthy, well-populated
terrarium will show none of them in a photo. Their absence from an image is not evidence of
anything, so don't remark on it either way.

**When the user tells you what's in their terrarium, take it as fact.** They know their own setup;
the photo is one input among several and the weaker one for this. If they say they have isopods and
springtails, that's settled — don't re-litigate it from pixels.

### Telling them apart from things that are genuinely a problem

"Small white things" is ambiguous and the answer matters, so discriminate on **where they are and
whether they move**:

| What | Size & look | Where | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Springtails** (קפיצזנבים) | ~1–2 mm, white/grey, **jump** when disturbed | Soil surface, glass near the substrate | ✅ Beneficial |
| **Isopods** — pill woodlice, *Armadillidiidae* (אורי כדורי) | 5–15 mm, grey/brown, segmented, **roll into a ball** | Under wood, leaf litter, hardscape | ✅ Beneficial. Land crustaceans, not insects |
| **Mealybugs** | 2–4 mm, white and **cottony/fuzzy**, immobile | **On the plant** — leaf axils, stems, undersides | ❌ Real pest, treat it |
| **Fungus gnat larvae** | Translucent white, black head, worm-like | **In** the soil | ⚠️ Symptom of soil kept too wet |
| **Whitefly** | ~2 mm, white winged, flies up when disturbed | On leaf undersides | ❌ Real pest |

**The discriminator that resolves most cases: on the soil and moving = cleanup crew; on the plant
and stationary and fuzzy = mealybug.** Springtails and mealybugs are both small and white, and
mistaking one for the other sends the answer in exactly the wrong direction — either treating a
healthy terrarium for an infestation, or ignoring a real one.

Other genuine terrarium pests: aphids or mites brought in on a new plant (mites are rare here —
high humidity suppresses them).

If the photo can't resolve which it is, say so and give the user the check to run themselves:
disturb them gently — springtails hop, isopods curl up, mealybugs don't move at all.

## 4. Diseases specific to the closed environment

High humidity plus still air is exactly the condition fungal pathogens want. This is why the
disease profile differs from a potted plant's — and why **airflow, not dryness, is usually the fix**.

| Problem | Look | Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Botrytis / grey mould** | Grey fuzzy growth on dying leaves and stems | Dead material + no airflow. The classic terrarium disease | Remove all dead tissue now, air out the vessel, prune for space |
| **White fuzzy mould on soil or wood** | Cottony white patches | Normal in a new build; usually resolves as it balances | Air out; springtails will clear it. Only intervene if it spreads to living tissue |
| **Damping off** | Seedlings or cuttings collapse at soil level | Fungal, in constantly saturated substrate | Reduce moisture, improve airflow, remove affected plants |
| **Rot at leaf contact points** | Soft dark patches where a leaf touches glass or another leaf | Water trapped against tissue | Prune to create space; wipe the glass |
| **Root/base rot** | Soft mushy base, yellowing from the bottom | Waterlogged substrate, flooded drainage layer | Air out; if the drainage layer is submerged, rebuild the terrarium |
| **Leggy stretched growth** | Long pale stems reaching upward | Too little light — glass and fog cut light more than people expect | Move to brighter indirect light, prune back |
| **Scorch / cooked plants** | Bleached or collapsed foliage, very high internal heat | **Direct sun** — glass creates a greenhouse and internal temperature spikes fast | Move out of direct sun immediately. This can kill in a single afternoon |
| **Overcrowding** | Plants pressed against glass and each other, lower leaves rotting | Normal growth in a small volume | Prune hard; it's maintenance, not disease |

**Powdery mildew is unusual here** — it prefers high humidity with still air but dislikes wet
surfaces, so in a terrarium botrytis usually gets there first. Don't reach for it by default.

## 5. Watering and seasonal advice for terrariums

**Closed terrarium:** give a *condition*, not an interval. "Water only if the glass shows no
condensation for several days and the plants look limp — otherwise nothing, possibly for months."
Adding water on a schedule is how these fail.

**Temper the venting advice to what's planted.** Airing out is the right fix for heavy fog, but some
terrarium favourites resent moving air and sustained low humidity — jewel orchids (*Macodes*,
*Anoectochilus*) are notably sensitive around both roots and leaves, and *Macodes* in particular
wants humidity at the high end. With those present, vent in short deliberate sessions to clear the
excess and then close it again, rather than leaving the vessel open for days or parking it in a
draught. The goal is to reach light droplets on the upper third, not to dry the thing out.

- Mist lightly when genuinely needed, a small amount at a time, then wait a day and reassess. It is
  far easier to add water than remove it.
- **Seasonal change is much flatter than for a potted plant** — the vessel buffers humidity, so the
  multipliers in `watering.md` mostly don't apply. What *does* change with season is **light**
  (shorter winter days may cause stretching) and **temperature** (a sunny winter windowsill can
  still overheat the glass, and a cold one can chill the roots).
- Routine maintenance beats watering: open it periodically to exchange air, remove dead leaves
  before they mould, and prune growth that's pressing on the glass.

**Open terrarium / bowl with no drainage hole:** treat as a pot with a serious caveat — there is no
way for excess water to leave. Water in small measured amounts (a fraction of what an equivalent
pot would take), aiming to dampen the substrate without filling the drainage layer. The seasonal
multipliers in `watering.md` do apply here. If it holds succulents, err dry, always.

## 6. What to say when the setup itself is the problem

Sometimes the honest diagnosis isn't a disease — it's that the terrarium is built wrong. Say it
plainly and constructively; it's more useful than a care tweak that can't work:

- Succulents or cacti sealed in a closed vessel → they will rot; move them out
- No drainage layer under the substrate → water has nowhere to go; needs rebuilding
- Vessel in direct sun → will overheat; move it
- Plants that have outgrown the vessel → prune or rehome, this is normal and expected
- Vessel sealed since day one with no airing → open it weekly
