# Watering, by plant type and season

Watering frequency is not a property of a species — it's a property of a species *in a situation*.
The same plant can need water twice a week in July and once a month in January. Giving a fixed
interval with no seasonal or environmental context is the most common way plant advice goes wrong,
and overwatering in winter kills more houseplants than any pest or disease.

**Always check the real current date (`date`) before advising.** Then work through: season →
growth phase → plant type → environment.

## 1. Season in Israel

Mediterranean climate — a long hot dry summer and a mild wet winter. Assume this unless the user's
context says otherwise; for other locations, adjust and say you've done so.

| Months | Season | What it means for watering |
|---|---|---|
| **April – June** | Spring → early summer | Growth accelerating; step frequency **up** gradually. Start fertilising |
| **July – August** | Peak summer | Maximum water use. Heat waves (*sharav*) can double it for a few days |
| **September – October** | Late summer, still hot | **Still summer watering.** The calendar says autumn well before the plants do — daylight shortens first, temperature follows in late October |
| **November – December** | Autumn → winter | Growth slowing; step frequency **down**. Stop fertilising |
| **January – February** | Winter | Minimum water use. Lowest light of the year |
| **March** | Early spring | Growth restarting; watch for new shoots and increase in response to them |

**Southern hemisphere:** shift six months. **Note this explicitly if the user isn't in Israel.**

## 2. Growth phase is what actually matters

The calendar is a proxy. What genuinely drives water use is whether the plant is growing, and that
is driven by **daylight hours and temperature**, which is why an indoor plant under a grow light on
a timer may barely slow down in winter while its neighbour on a windowsill stops completely.

Read the plant, not just the month:
- **New leaves unfurling, visible extension** → actively growing, water on the growing-season schedule
- **No new growth, existing leaves stable** → resting; stretch the interval
- **Dying back, leaves yellowing and dropping in autumn, nothing new** → going dormant; cut water
  drastically (see §5 — this is not a disease and must not be treated as one)

Someone running grow lights ~12h/day year-round keeps their plants closer to growing-season needs
through winter than the season alone would suggest — but light is only half of it, so still reduce,
just less sharply.

## 3. Base intervals by plant type

Growing season (spring/summer), average indoor conditions, correctly-sized pot. Treat as a starting
point to be confirmed by the finger test (§7), never as a schedule to follow blindly.

| Type | Examples | Interval | Let it dry to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cacti & desert succulents** | Echeveria, aloe, most cacti | 14–21 days | Bone dry throughout |
| **Semi-succulent** | Sansevieria, ZZ, jade, hoya | 10–18 days | Dry two thirds down |
| **Thick-leaved aroids** | Monstera, philodendron, syngonium | 7–10 days | Top 3–5 cm dry |
| **Thin-leaved tropicals** | Calathea, maranta, fittonia, begonia | 4–7 days | Top 1–2 cm dry, never fully |
| **Ferns** | Boston, maidenhair, asparagus | 3–5 days | Consistently lightly moist |
| **Tuberous aroids** | **Caladium**, alocasia, colocasia | 4–7 days *in growth* | Top 2–3 cm dry — and see §5 |
| **Citrus & woody potted** | Lemon, olive, ficus | 7–14 days | Top 5 cm dry |
| **Herbs** | Basil, mint, parsley | 2–4 days | Top 1–2 cm dry |
| **Orchids (bark mix)** | Phalaenopsis | 7–10 days | Roots turn silvery-green |

Small pots and terracotta dry much faster than large pots and glazed or plastic ones — a 10 cm
terracotta pot can need water twice as often as a 25 cm plastic one holding the same species.

## 4. Seasonal adjustment

Multiply the growing-season interval:

| Season | Multiplier | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Peak summer (Jul–Aug), or a heat wave | **×0.7** | Noticeably more often |
| Growing season (Apr–Oct) | **×1** | Base interval |
| Autumn transition (Nov–Dec) | **×1.5** | Start stretching |
| Winter (Jan–Feb) | **×2** | Roughly half as often |
| Winter, **dormant** species | see §5 | Nearly nothing, or nothing at all |

Example: a monstera on 7–10 days in summer goes to roughly 14–20 days in January. A caladium doesn't
scale like this at all — it stops.

**Fertiliser follows the same curve:** feed during active growth, taper in autumn, stop entirely in
winter. Fertilising a dormant or barely-growing plant builds up salts it isn't using, which shows
up later as brown leaf margins.

## 5. Dormancy table

For these, seasonal adjustment isn't a tweak — it decides whether the plant survives the winter.
Watering a dormant tuber on a summer schedule rots it, and that rot is usually only discovered in
spring when nothing comes up.

| Plant | Dormancy behaviour | Winter watering |
|---|---|---|
| **Caladium** | Leaves yellow and die back completely in autumn — **normal, not disease** | Taper as the leaves yellow, then stop completely once they've died back. Rest the tuber dry — in the pot in a cool dry spot, or lifted and stored at 13–18 °C. Resume light watering in spring; shoots appear 2–4 weeks after the soil warms |
| **Alocasia** | Partial die-back, may drop to one or two leaves | Water sparingly, only when quite dry |
| **Caudex & deciduous succulents** | Drop leaves entirely | Little to none until growth resumes |
| **Amaryllis, tuberous begonia, gloxinia** | Full die-back | Dry rest period, then restart |
| **Most cacti** | Rest, no growth | Once a month at most; many keepers give none at all below 10 °C |
| **Oxalis** | Foliage collapses | Rest dry, regrows from bulbs |

**When a caladium yellows and collapses in autumn, the correct advice is to reduce water and let it
go dormant — not to diagnose a disease.** This is the single most common misreading of a seasonal
plant, and getting it right is a large part of why this skill checks the date.

## 6. Environmental modifiers

Adjust the interval for the actual room, not an average one:

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| Air conditioning or winter heating running | Air dries fast → more humidity need, but soil use rises only slightly. **Don't confuse the two** |
| Low light (winter, north-facing) | Much slower water use → longer interval |
| Grow lights 10–14 h/day | Keeps growth going → shorter interval than the season implies |
| Terracotta pot | Dries much faster than plastic or glazed |
| Oversized pot | Stays wet far longer → serious rot risk; a common hidden cause |
| No drainage hole | Water pools invisibly at the bottom. Water very sparingly, or repot |
| Rootbound | Very little soil left to hold water → dries fast, needs repotting more than watering |

**The humidity trap:** dry air causes crispy leaf tips, and the instinct is to water more. But dry
air is a humidity problem and wet soil is a root problem — adding water to the soil doesn't fix the
air and does risk rot. Raise humidity (pebble tray, humidifier, grouping plants) and leave the
watering interval alone.

## 7. How to test soil moisture

Always give the test alongside the interval. The interval sets expectations; the test keeps it
right when the weather turns.

- **Finger test** — push a finger 3–5 cm into the soil. Cool and damp: wait. Dry and loose: water.
  The most reliable method available to anyone, and better than any schedule.
- **Weight test** — lift the pot just after watering and again when dry; the difference is
  unmistakable. Best for large plants where fingers can't reach the root zone.
- **Wooden skewer** — push it deep, pull it out. Clinging damp soil means wait. Works where fingers
  don't reach.
- **Moisture meter** — convenient but drifts and reads oddly in bark or very loose mixes; treat as
  a hint, not an authority.
- **Soil pulling away from the pot edge** — already too dry. Water slowly so it doesn't just run
  down the gap.

## 8. Over- vs under-watering

These look alarmingly alike — both wilt, both brown, both yellow. **Distinguish by the soil, not
the leaves.**

| Sign | Overwatered | Underwatered |
|---|---|---|
| Soil | Wet, possibly sour-smelling | Dry, pulled from pot edge |
| Pot weight | Heavy | Light |
| Leaf texture | Soft, limp, sometimes translucent | Crispy, dry, brittle |
| Yellowing | Common, spreading generally | Less common; browning first |
| Stem base | May be soft, dark, mushy | Firm |
| After watering | No improvement, or worse | Recovers within hours |
| Soil surface | May grow mould or algae | Dusty, crusted |

**Wilting with wet soil means root rot, not thirst.** Roots damaged by standing water can't take up
water even though it's there — so the plant wilts *because* it's been overwatered, and watering
again accelerates the collapse. Whenever a wilted plant sits in wet soil, check the roots (§7 of
`diseases.md`) before doing anything else.

## 9. Watering technique

- Water **thoroughly** until it runs from the drainage holes, then let it drain fully. Frequent
  small sips only wet the top, leaving deep roots dry and encouraging shallow rooting.
- **Empty the saucer** after 15–30 minutes. A pot standing in water is the standard route to rot.
- Water the **soil, not the leaves**, especially in the evening — wet foliage overnight invites
  fungal and bacterial spots (see `diseases.md` §1).
- Water in the **morning** where possible, so the plant uses it through the day.
- Tap water is fine for most plants. Calathea, maranta, dracaena and spider plants are sensitive to
  fluoride and chlorine and get brown tips from it — for those, use filtered or stood-out water.
- Room-temperature water; cold water shocks tropical roots.
