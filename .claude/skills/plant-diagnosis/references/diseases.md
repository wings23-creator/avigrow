# Symptom → cause reference

Use this to turn an observation into a named cause. Every section leads with **how to tell it apart
from the things it's most often confused with**, because that discrimination is the hard part —
"brown leaf tips" has six common causes that need different treatments, and picking the wrong one
wastes weeks.

## 1. Leaf spots and lesions

**The key discrimination is fungal vs bacterial vs physical**, and the margin of the spot tells you:

| Feature | Fungal | Bacterial | Physical/scorch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Round, defined | Angular — stops at leaf veins | Irregular, follows contact/exposure |
| Margin | Crisp, often with a concentric ring or dark border | Water-soaked, greasy, translucent when backlit | Dry, brittle, no halo |
| Halo | Sometimes yellow | Often bright yellow, wide | None |
| Texture | Dry, may show tiny black dots (fruiting bodies) | Wet, may ooze or smell | Papery |
| Spread | Slow, spreads in humidity | Fast, spreads with splashing water | Doesn't spread |

**Angular spots that stop dead at the veins are the single most reliable bacterial sign** — the
bacteria can't cross the vein. Round spots that cross veins freely are fungal.

- **Anthracnose** — dark sunken spots, often starting at leaf tip and spreading back along the
  midrib; common on ficus, dracaena, palms. Remove affected leaves, reduce leaf wetness.
- **Leaf spot (Cercospora, Colletotrichum)** — brown/tan spots with darker borders and a yellow
  halo. Improve air circulation, avoid overhead watering, remove affected leaves.
- **Bacterial leaf spot / blight (Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas)** — angular water-soaked lesions,
  spreads fast in warm wet conditions. **Aroids (caladium, philodendron, anthurium, syngonium) are
  particularly prone.** Isolate the plant immediately, stop misting, remove affected leaves with a
  sterilised blade. Bacterial infections are not curable with fungicide.
- **Rust** — raised orange/brown pustules, usually on the leaf underside, that smudge orange on a
  finger. Remove leaves, don't compost them.
- **Viral mosaic** — mottled light/dark patches in an irregular mosaic, often with leaf distortion.
  **Check first whether the species is naturally variegated** — caladium, calathea, aglaonema and
  many others are patterned by nature and constantly get misdiagnosed as viral. Viral mottling is
  usually accompanied by distortion and stunting; healthy variegation is not. No cure; discard.

## 2. Yellowing (chlorosis)

**The pattern matters more than the colour.**

| Pattern | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| Uniform yellowing of **oldest/lowest** leaves | Nitrogen deficiency, or natural leaf ageing (a leaf or two at a time is normal) |
| Uniform yellowing spreading generally, soil wet | Overwatering / root rot — the classic presentation |
| **Interveinal** (veins stay green, tissue between yellows) on **new** leaves | Iron deficiency, usually from soil pH too high, not from absence of iron |
| **Interveinal** on **old** leaves | Magnesium deficiency |
| Yellow with dark green veins + stunted growth | Manganese or zinc deficiency |
| Sudden yellow + drop after a move | Transplant/relocation shock — not a disease |
| Yellowing only on the light-facing side | Sunscald |

**Old leaves vs new leaves is the diagnostic axis** for deficiencies. Mobile nutrients (nitrogen,
magnesium, potassium) get pulled out of old leaves to feed new growth, so deficiency shows on old
leaves first. Immobile nutrients (iron, calcium, manganese) can't be moved, so deficiency shows on
new growth first. This one distinction resolves most nutrient questions.

## 3. Browning and dead tissue (necrosis)

| Where | Cause | Distinguishing detail |
|---|---|---|
| Tips only, thin brown line | Low humidity | Most common indoor cause by far; especially calathea, maranta, fern, caladium |
| Tips + edges, wider band | Salt/fertiliser buildup or fluoridated tap water | Often with white crust on soil or pot rim; flush the pot thoroughly |
| Edges, crispy, whole leaf limp | Underwatering | Soil pulls away from the pot edge; pot feels light |
| Edges, soft/dark rather than crispy | Overwatering | Confusingly similar — check soil weight and smell, not the leaf |
| Large irregular patches mid-leaf | Sunscald or grow-light burn | Only on light-exposed surfaces; sharply bounded |
| Spreading from the base/crown | Rot — treat urgently | See §7 |
| Whole leaf browns and drops fast | Cold damage / draught | Follows a cold snap or an open window |

**Crispy vs soft is the fastest over- vs under-watering discriminator on leaf damage** — but confirm
with the soil, not the leaf, because both extremes cause wilting and browning.

## 4. Wilting and drooping

| Pattern | Cause |
|---|---|
| Uniform droop, soil dry, pot light | Underwatering — recovers within hours of watering |
| Uniform droop, soil **wet** | Root rot — roots can't take up water even though it's there. Does not recover from watering; watering more makes it worse |
| One stem or one side wilts, rest fine | Vascular disease (fusarium/verticillium) or physical stem damage |
| Wilts in afternoon, recovers overnight | Heat stress — normal in high summer, not a disease |
| Sudden collapse of a previously fine plant | Crown rot, stem borer, or severe root loss |

**Wilting with wet soil is the most misread symptom in houseplant care** — it looks exactly like
thirst, and the instinctive response (more water) kills the plant. When soil is wet and the plant
wilts, check the roots.

## 5. Deformed, curled, or stunted growth

- **New growth distorted, silvery/stippled scarring** → thrips. Look for tiny black frass specks.
- **New growth curled and cupped, sticky** → aphids. Check tight new shoots and the undersides.
- **Leaves cupping upward** → heat or light stress, or underwatering.
- **Leaves curling downward/inward** → overwatering, or root damage.
- **Long pale stems, wide gaps between leaves, small leaves** → etiolation from insufficient light.
- **New leaves smaller than old ones, generally pale** → rootbound, nutrient exhaustion, or too
  little light. Check for roots at the drainage holes.
- **Growth stops entirely with no other symptom, in autumn/winter** → normal seasonal dormancy.
  Check `watering.md` before treating this as a problem.

## 6. Pests

Most live on **leaf undersides, leaf axils, and new growth** — the places a whole-plant photo
doesn't show. Say so when the photo can't rule them out.

| Pest | Visible sign | Damage pattern | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spider mites** | Fine webbing at leaf axils and tips; specks that move | Fine pale stippling, whole leaf goes dull bronze; thrives in dry heat | Raise humidity, rinse thoroughly, insecticidal soap or neem; repeat weekly ×3 |
| **Thrips** | Slender dark specks; black frass dots | Silvery/bronze scarring, distorted new growth | Blue sticky traps, neem, remove badly hit leaves; persistent — expect several rounds |
| **Mealybugs** | White cottony clusters in axils and under leaves | Yellowing, sticky honeydew, stunting | Dab with 70% alcohol on a swab, then insecticidal soap; check the root ball too |
| **Scale** | Small brown/tan immobile bumps on stems and veins | Yellow spots, honeydew, sooty mould | Scrape off, then horticultural oil; the immobile shell resists sprays |
| **Aphids** | Green/black clusters on new shoots | Curled sticky new growth | Rinse off, insecticidal soap |
| **Fungus gnats** | Small black flies around the soil | Larvae damage fine roots; mostly a symptom of soil kept too wet | Let soil dry between waterings, yellow sticky traps, top-dress with sand |
| **Whitefly** | Tiny white flies that scatter when disturbed | Yellow stippling, honeydew | Sticky traps, insecticidal soap |

**Sticky leaves are diagnostic.** Honeydew means a sap-sucking insect (aphid, scale, mealybug,
whitefly) — even if you can't see it. Sooty mould, the black film that grows on honeydew, means
the same. Treat the insect; the mould leaves on its own.

**Stippling vs scarring** separates the two most-confused pests: mites make fine even *dots*
(individual feeding punctures); thrips make *streaks and patches* with a silvery metallic sheen.

## 7. Root and crown problems

- **Root rot** — Soil wet, plant wilts, base soft or mushy, soil smells sour or sulphurous. Roots
  are brown/black and slimy; healthy roots are firm and pale. **This is the most common killer of
  indoor plants.** Act now: unpot, cut away all soft roots with a sterilised blade, repot into
  fresh dry free-draining mix, water sparingly until new growth appears. Do not fertilise a
  recovering plant.
- **Rootbound** — Roots circling the inside of the pot or coming out the drainage holes; water runs
  straight through; plant dries out unusually fast; new leaves shrinking. Pot up one size (not
  more — an oversized pot holds wet soil and causes rot).
- **Salt/mineral buildup** — White or yellow crust on the soil surface or pot rim; brown leaf
  edges. Flush with several pot-volumes of water, or repot.
- **Crown/tuber rot** — Particularly relevant to tuberous plants (caladium, alocasia, begonia) kept
  wet during dormancy. The growing point turns soft and dark. Usually fatal once visible; prevent
  it by keeping dormant tubers nearly dry.

## 8. Powdery, fuzzy, and sooty coatings

| Look | Cause | Note |
|---|---|---|
| White powder, wipes off, on upper leaf surface | Powdery mildew | Thrives in high humidity with **still air** — ventilation matters more than dryness |
| Grey/brown fuzz on dying tissue | Botrytis (grey mould) | Remove affected tissue immediately, increase airflow |
| Black sooty film, wipes off, leaves sticky | Sooty mould on honeydew | Not a pathogen — find and treat the insect (§6) |
| White crust that does **not** wipe off | Mineral/salt deposit | See §7 |
| Fuzzy white on soil surface | Harmless saprophytic mould | Indicates soil stays too wet; scrape off, water less |

## 9. Nutrient deficiencies

Reach for this **only after ruling out watering and light**, which cause the same symptoms far more
often. A plant in soggy soil can't absorb nutrients even when they're present, so fertilising a
waterlogged plant makes things worse, not better.

| Nutrient | Shows on | Symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | Old leaves | Uniform pale yellowing, weak growth |
| Phosphorus (P) | Old leaves | Dark dull green, sometimes purple tinge, stunted |
| Potassium (K) | Old leaves | Yellow/brown scorched margins, weak stems |
| Magnesium (Mg) | Old leaves | Interveinal yellowing, veins stay sharply green |
| Iron (Fe) | **New** leaves | Interveinal yellowing on youngest growth; usually a pH problem |
| Calcium (Ca) | **New** leaves | Deformed, hooked, or dying growing tips |
| Manganese (Mn) | New leaves | Interveinal yellowing with fine dark speckling |

## 10. Physical and environmental damage

Not disease, and treating it as disease wastes effort. Distinguishing sign: **it doesn't spread**
and it maps to an obvious exposure.

- **Grow-light or sun scorch** — bleached or brown patches only on surfaces facing the light,
  sharply bounded, worst on the leaves nearest the lamp. Increase the lamp distance.
- **Cold/draught damage** — sudden blackening or translucent patches after a cold night or near an
  air-conditioner vent or open window.
- **Mechanical damage** — tears, creases, scrapes with clean edges and no halo; often from handling,
  transport, or a pet brushing past.
- **Edema** — small corky blisters on the underside, from overwatering in low light with poor
  airflow. Harmless; fix the watering.
- **Natural leaf ageing** — the oldest leaf yellowing and dropping while everything else grows
  normally is not a problem. Say so plainly rather than inventing a cause.

## 11. Severity triage

Rank the recommendation by how fast it must happen:

- **Urgent (today)** — root or crown rot, bacterial infection spreading, heavy active pest
  infestation. Isolate the plant from others first, then act.
- **This week** — early pest signs, salt buildup, rootbound, fungal leaf spot.
- **Adjust and observe** — humidity, light distance, watering interval, fertilising.
- **Nothing needed** — natural ageing, seasonal dormancy, normal variegation, cosmetic mechanical
  damage. Saying "this is fine, here's why" is a real and useful answer.
