# Reading the light before reading the plant

The goal here is **not** to disclaim your way out of a diagnosis. It is to work out what light the
photo was taken under, mentally correct for it, and then still give a precise answer — while being
clear about the few judgments the light genuinely destroys. A photo under a purple grow light is
perfectly diagnosable once you know it's a purple grow light. What ruins the answer is not noticing.

## Step 1: classify the light source

Identify which of these you're looking at. The cues are strong and quick — this takes seconds.

| Light source | Tell-tale signs in the photo | Effect on the plant's apparent colour |
|---|---|---|
| **"Blurple" LED grow light** (red+blue diodes) | Everything neutral in frame — white pot, floor tile, wall, ceiling — is tinted magenta/violet. No pure green anywhere. Shadows are violet, not grey. Often a visible pink glow gradient or LED strip. Yellows go muddy brown (these lamps have a spectral gap in yellow-green) | **Pinks and reds massively exaggerated.** Greens flatten toward grey-olive or mauve. Silvery/white variegation picks up a lilac tint |
| **Full-spectrum white grow light** | Bright, fairly neutral, but with a faint magenta or blue lean in shadows and on white surfaces | Mild. Slight over-saturation; usually correctable to near-accurate |
| **Warm incandescent / warm LED bulb** (evening indoors) | Everything leans yellow-orange. Whites look cream. Warm pools of light with dark falloff | **Greens shift toward yellow — this fakes chlorosis.** The single most common cause of a false "your plant is yellowing" reading |
| **Cool fluorescent / office light** | Slight green-cyan cast, flat and shadowless | Mild; can make healthy leaves look sickly grey-green |
| **Daylight, indirect** | Neutral whites, soft directional shadows, visible window light | **The reference condition.** Colour readable at face value |
| **Direct sun** | Hard-edged black shadows, blown-out highlights, high contrast | Colour fine in midtones; burnt-out highlights are unreadable, deep shadows hide detail |
| **Camera flash at night** | Harsh specular highlights, bright subject, background falling to black | Slightly cool and over-saturated; strong glare spots on glossy leaves |

If several apply, you're in [mixed light](#mixed-light--the-tricky-one).

## Step 2: find a neutral reference and measure the cast

Look for something in the frame that **should** be neutral white or grey:

- a white or black plastic pot, a saucer
- floor tile, grout lines, a painted wall or ceiling
- a nursery label, a plant tag, paper
- white perlite specks in the potting mix (excellent — they're right next to the leaves, under
  exactly the same light)
- bare metal — a plant stake, a stand, a hinge

**How far that object sits from true neutral is the size and direction of the cast.** Perlite that
photographs lilac tells you the whole image is pushed toward magenta by roughly that much. This is
the same thing a camera's white balance does, done by eye.

If nothing neutral is in frame, fall back to the light-source classification in Step 1 and say your
correction is estimated rather than referenced.

---

## Step 3: correct, then read

Subtract the measured cast from the plant, then interpret:

- **Under magenta/purple:** pull the pink way down and the green up. A leaf that photographs hot
  fuchsia is usually a normal mid-pink; one that photographs grey-mauve is usually ordinary green.
  Ask "what would this be under daylight?" and diagnose *that*, not what's on screen.
- **Under warm/incandescent:** subtract yellow-orange. Apparent mild chlorosis very often
  disappears entirely once corrected — check this before ever calling yellowing at night indoors.
- **Under cool/fluorescent:** add back a little warmth; grey-green usually resolves to healthy green.

State the correction you applied in one short phrase, so the reasoning is visible and the user can
tell you if you misjudged the lamp. Then give a real diagnosis.

## Day vs night

Time of day changes both what's reliable and what's normal for the plant.

**Reading the photo:**
- **Daytime** — daylight through a window is neutral and mixes with any lamp, diluting its cast.
  Backgrounds are evenly lit. Colour is broadly trustworthy.
- **Night** — the only light is artificial, so the cast is at full strength and there is no daylight
  to dilute it. Backgrounds go dark, falloff is steep, and flash glare is common. **Apply the
  correction more aggressively at night**, and be correspondingly more careful with colour calls.
- Tells for night: dark or black windows, pooled lighting with dark surroundings, strong lamp
  gradients, flash highlights.

**Reading the plant:**
Some plants genuinely look different at night, and this is normal behaviour, not a symptom:
- **Nyctinasty** — prayer plants (*Maranta*, *Calathea*, *Stromanthe*, *Ctenanthe*), oxalis, and
  many legumes fold their leaves upward at night. A night photo of a calathea with leaves folded
  vertically shows a healthy plant doing exactly what it should. Never diagnose this as wilting.
- Many flowers close at night; some open only at night.
- Slight evening droop in large-leaved plants after a hot day is normal turgor recovery, not a
  problem, if the plant is upright again by morning.

If a photo shows folded or drooping leaves at night, ask whether the plant looks the same in the
morning before treating it as a symptom — that single question separates normal rhythm from real
water stress.

## Mixed light — the tricky one

Window light on one side plus a lamp on the other is common and easy to misread: **the two sides of
the plant will genuinely look like different colours.** That is a lighting artefact, not a symptom.

Rule: before calling any left/right or front/back colour difference a symptom, check whether it
maps to the lighting geometry. If the "affected" side is the side facing the lamp, or facing away
from the window, it's the light. Real symptoms cluster by leaf age, by position on the leaf, or by
proximity to soil — not by which way the plant faces a lamp.

The exception that proves the rule: damage **is** genuinely light-related when it appears only on
surfaces nearest a lamp and is bleached or brown rather than merely differently-hued — that's
scorch (see `diseases.md` §10), and the giveaway is that it's sharply bounded and doesn't wash out
when you correct the colour.

## What survives a colour cast and what doesn't

This is the part that lets you stay precise instead of hedging everything.

**Fully reliable under any light** — read these confidently:
- Shape, outline, leaf margin, venation pattern, leaf arrangement, growth habit
- Texture, gloss, wrinkling, puckering
- Turgor: upright vs limp vs drooping
- Holes, tears, notches, chew marks
- Spot and lesion **geometry** — round vs angular, sharp vs diffuse margin, whether they stop at
  veins, concentric rings
- Webbing, cottony masses, scale bumps, visible insects, frass
- Soil surface texture, perlite, crusting, moisture sheen
- Position and distribution of damage — tips vs margins vs midrib, old leaves vs new

**Reliable as a relative comparison, even under a strong cast** — this is the key trick: every leaf
in one photo sits under the *same* light, so comparisons within the photo hold even when absolute
colour doesn't. "This leaf is markedly yellower than that one", "the margin is darker than the
centre", "the new growth is paler than the old" are all valid, and most colour-based diagnosis is
really a comparison anyway. Use this rather than giving up on colour.

**Genuinely unreliable — say so plainly:**
- Absolute hue and saturation ("how pink is it really")
- Subtle or early chlorosis
- Distinguishing silvering from bleaching from natural variegation
- Judging whether a variegated cultivar's colours are "good" for that cultivar
- Fine colour detail inside blown-out highlights or crushed shadows

## Ignore the background

The plant is the subject. **The room is evidence, never content.**

Use the background silently, for three things only:
1. a neutral reference for white balance (Step 2)
2. identifying the light source and time of day (Step 1)
3. the pot, soil surface, stake, and saucer — these are genuinely part of the diagnosis

Beyond that, don't mention it. No furniture, no floor, no walls, no screens, no other rooms, no
people or animals, no describing the setting. If a second plant is visible and clearly relevant
(same species showing the same symptom, or a neighbouring plant with an obvious infestation that
could spread), one sentence is warranted — otherwise leave it out. Mentally crop to the plant and
its pot, and answer about that.

## How to phrase it

Fold the lighting into the answer briefly and move on. It's a premise, not a topic — one or two
lines, then get to the diagnosis.

**Good:** "צולם תחת גרואו-לייט סגול (הרצפה והפרלייט יוצאים לילכיים) — תיקנתי את הוורוד כלפי מטה
בקריאה. הזיהוי והמבנה לא מושפעים; מה שלא אוכל לקבוע מהתמונה הזו זה גוון עדין, למשל האם יש הצהבה
קלה מתחילה."

**Bad:** a whole paragraph about colour theory, followed by refusing to diagnose. The user wants to
know what's wrong with the plant.

**Also bad:** silently diagnosing colour through a magenta cast as though it were daylight. That is
how a healthy green plant gets reported as reddening, or a warm-lit evening photo produces a
confident diagnosis of nitrogen deficiency.
